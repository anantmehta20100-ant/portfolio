Prototype scope

TrackSense does not chemically detect allergens, confirm that contamination has occurred, or confirm that food is safe. It produces prototype relative-risk estimates from observed object interactions; physical verification and appropriate food-safety procedures remain necessary.

Overview

TrackSense combines object detection, tracking, persistent contact monitoring, risk propagation, a Flask API, and a live dashboard in one completed end-to-end prototype.

{{ media_placeholder("TrackSense dashboard image coming soon") }}

Problem

Possible cross-contact can travel indirectly through shared objects. A useful prototype therefore needs to reason about interaction history rather than classify one camera frame in isolation.

Core insight

An elevated-risk plate may be connected to the original source through cutlery and bread even when the plate never directly touches the source.

System architecture

{{ architecture_diagram() }}

Detection system

YOLO through Ultralytics detects eight relevant classes: nut butter jar, whole nuts, hand, cutlery, chopping board, plate, bowl, and bread.

The eight-class schema

I locked this schema before preparing any data, choosing classes that covered exactly the nut-transfer scenario and no more. Fixing the class IDs early meant data preparation, detection, contact logic, and the dashboard could all rely on the same stable set. I also dropped a planned counter class after finding that wood-grain surfaces caused false detections.

One shared camera

The whole system is built around a single camera and one continuous detection loop, with the capture shared so that one frame source feeds both the live detector and the contact monitor. I learned early that opening the camera more than once makes the components fight over the device, so sharing a single capture became a deliberate design constraint rather than an optimisation.

Object tracking

Custom IoU tracking associates detections across frames without hiding the tracking logic behind a large external framework.

Contact tracking

Bounding-box overlap, normalized distance, and multi-frame persistence reduce one-frame contact noise. The monitor checks the current detections four times a second and looks for objects whose boxes overlap; when two objects are in contact and one already carries risk, that risk passes to the other and moves one step further along the chain. The value passed along is always a relative estimate of how likely exposure is, never a measured quantity of allergen.

Risk propagation

A deterministic heuristic engine and a Random Forest risk classifier support explainable prototype scoring and baseline comparison.

Dataset engineering

Roboflow, COCO-to-YOLO remapping, dataset consolidation, class validation, Google Colab, and GPU training supported the data workflow. No single public dataset matched my eight-class schema, so I built the training data by collapsing several external datasets down into my own classes — mapping many different nut subcategories onto the single whole_nuts class, building the nut classes to a validated one-to-one balance, and standardising every class ID to the canonical schema. I kept further COCO-format datasets covering nuts, peanuts, kitchen utensils, general kitchen objects, and bread as a separate insurance track, so experiments could never interfere with the working model.

Risk-model dataset and split

The risk model needs its own training data, so I generated a set of contact-event scenarios and split them by scenario rather than by row, so that no scenario could leak between training and testing. The figures below are from the real training run.

Co-detection failure

The model had seen many objects in isolated, single-class training images. It therefore struggled to detect several relevant classes together in realistic scenes.

Root-cause analysis

Testing and dataset analysis connected the runtime failure to the composition of the training data rather than treating it as an unexplained model weakness.

Synthetic data solution

Synthetic multi-object scenes were generated to strengthen co-detection and make realistic combinations part of the training distribution.

Results

The completed prototype provides live object detection, contact tracking, risk propagation, dashboard output, and automated tests. Some object classes performed more strongly than others, and further real-world validation is required. The pipeline itself worked end to end — continuous detection from a single shared camera, the full set of endpoints responding, the contact monitor polling four times a second, the honesty constraint enforced throughout, and the test suite passing.

Detection reliability by object

The honest limitation was not the pipeline but how reliably the detector recognised real physical objects. A food-safety tool that hides its own unreliability is worse than useless, so the weak classes are recorded here rather than left out.

Staging the demonstration honestly

Rather than pretend the weak classes worked, I staged the live demonstration around the strong ones: the nut butter jar, metal cutlery, bread, and a plate on a plain, non-wood surface. That single choice avoids every known weak point at once — the unreliable classes are simply not in the scene, metal cutlery replaces plastic, and the plain surface removes the wood-grain false positives. I also kept a headless recording of the full pipeline as a backup so the demonstration would hold up if the live setup misbehaved.

What I would do next

The clearest improvement is the detector itself. I would retrain on the weak classes — whole_nuts, bowl, and plastic cutlery — using the extra datasets I had already gathered, and give the model more epochs and more data, since those classes suffered most from having too few examples. I would also add proper counter-surface classes to separate a chopping board from a work surface cleanly. I deliberately left this retrain until after the live demonstration so that experimenting could never disturb the model I needed to show.

Privacy by design

The camera is not used to collect data. TrackSense reads the live video only to work out which objects are present and what is touching what; it does not save, store, or record images or footage of the people in the kitchen. Everything runs locally on a single laptop, so the video never has to leave the room. A tool meant to protect people should not create a new privacy risk of its own.
